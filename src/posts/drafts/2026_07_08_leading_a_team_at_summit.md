I never set out to manage anyone. I ended up leading a six-person engineering team anyway, and somewhere in the last few years the accidental part of that stopped being true. These are a few of the moments that actually changed how I think about it, and the principles I only noticed after they'd already happened.

## Make yourself replaceable, not indispensable

For years I was the person a system couldn't run without, which sounds like a compliment until you notice it also means every decision, every "why does this break," every PR routes through you whether or not it actually needs you specifically. That's not leverage. It's a single point of failure wearing a nice job title.

The fix wasn't working harder or writing more documentation, though I did plenty of both. It was handing off clean, well-scoped problem definitions instead of half-formed ones, so the people on my team could actually own a domain end to end instead of routing every decision back through me. Three of five engineers on my team now run their own piece of the platform, start to finish. I still own every code review. That's the job now: not knowing everything, but making sure nothing ships that shouldn't.

## Translate, don't gatekeep

A leadership change in 2024 left me as the only technical partner to a CTO who wasn't a developer. My first instinct was to protect him from complexity he didn't need, which is a nice way of saying I almost decided things for him instead of with him.

What actually worked was slower and less satisfying in the moment: translating system realities into terms he could act on, and trusting him to make the call once he actually understood the tradeoff. The job wasn't being the only person who understood the system. It was making sure someone who didn't have my background could still make a good decision about it.

## Growth comes from questions, not answers

I spent nine months coaching an engineer through a Vue 2 to Vue 3 migration, and the version of that story that would make me look smartest is the one where I designed the architecture and he executed it. That's not what happened. I asked questions instead, including some I already knew the answer to, and let him make the actual calls, including adopting a component library the team still owns because he chose it, not because I assigned it.

I've run a mob-programming meetup for developers from non-traditional backgrounds for going on seven years now, and the thing I keep re-learning there is the same thing: the question that unlocks someone's understanding is usually worth more than the answer would have been. It's just easier to remember that with strangers at a meetup than with someone on your own team when the deadline is real.

## Trust is earned by removing friction, not adding features

Our sales team had a customer account page they'd quietly stopped using in live demos, because pulling it up took close to ten minutes. Nobody had filed a bug. They'd just stopped trying.

A relational redesign got that page under a minute. The team started using it live to close contracts again, without being asked to and without a training session. Nobody trusts a tool because it has more features. They trust it because it stops being in their way.

## Someone still has to fix it, even without a plan

Twice in the last few years I inherited a mess that wasn't mine: a board-mandated CMS migration left without an owner after the person who scoped it left, and an SEO collapse after a disruptive top-down rollout I didn't choose. Neither situation had a villain worth naming, and looking for one wouldn't have shipped anything. What actually mattered was picking the thing up, fixing it without a lot of noise about whose fault it was, and letting the result speak for itself once it was done.

That's most of what I think leadership actually is, once you strip out the parts that look good in a slide deck. Not being the smartest person in the room. Making sure the room doesn't need you to be.
